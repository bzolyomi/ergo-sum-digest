Consciousness and moral status

Nagel's Berggruen Prize prompted a wave of commentary on whether AI systems could be conscious. The commentary I can see mostly reads him as skeptical, with the reasoning summarized as 'no body, no consciousness'. Others argue that uncertainty is the honest position, since we understand so little about consciousness even in animals, infants and hypothetical aliens, and that human-like machine minds raise the question anyway. On the academic side, a philpapers paper applies higher-order theories (a mind is conscious only if it represents its own mental states) to AI. Consciousness talk as a question of power

Naaman and Pasquale argue that AI is 'an artifact — made, not born' and that granting rights to machines would shift power to the companies that make and control them. Pasquale's framing treats the AI consciousness discourse as a slippery slope toward more corporate power. This is a normative and political argument rather than a metaphysical one. It deserves attention because it asks who gains from the consciousness question being taken seriously, not whether the answer is yes.

Power and economics beyond GDP

Two items ask what actually determines human standing and welfare as AI advances. One argues that military relevance matters more than economic relevance: historically, serfs were economically necessary but coercible, while aristocrats did no productive work yet held power through force. Tyler Cowen argues that AI's gains in customization and product variety are not captured by GDP. Both challenge the usual way of measuring AI's impact.

Culture and thinking

Medical education and professional identity

The medical education items worry that frictionless AI undermines the effort that builds clinical judgment. 'Effortless excellence' covers automation bias, cognitive offloading, deskilling and never-skilling. 'Screen retreat' describes trainees turning to the EMR rather than the bedside.
